E-Discovery Essentials: your first review decision.

A five-minute microlearning lesson for people new to document review. Learners apply written review instructions to realistic emails, learn that relevance is not the same as proof of wrongdoing, and practice flagging a document for guidance instead of guessing.

See the review activity ↓
Audience
New document reviewers and new hires on legal or review teams
Format
One 5-minute microlesson, two decisions
Tools
Articulate Rise 360
My role
Sole designer and developer
The performance problem

New reviewers confuse "relevant" with "proves something" and guess when unsure.

In document review, a reviewer applies written instructions to decide whether each document relates to the matter. Two early mistakes are common: treating relevance as if it meant proof of wrongdoing, and making a confident call on a document the instructions don't clearly cover.

Both lead to inconsistent coding that someone has to find and fix later. The lesson targets the judgment call itself, not the software.

Learning objectives

What learners should be able to do

  1. Describe e-discovery and where document review fits in it.
  2. Apply written review instructions to classify a document as Relevant or Not relevant, based on content and context rather than a matching keyword.
  3. Recognize when the instructions don't clearly apply, and flag the document for guidance instead of guessing.
Course flow

Five minutes, two decisions

  1. Hook: "Can you identify a relevant message, and recognize when you need guidance?"
  2. Context: what e-discovery is, and what review means.
  3. Assignment: a fictional investigation with written review instructions.
  4. Decision 1: a clearly relevant email, followed by the takeaway "relevance does not mean wrongdoing."
  5. Decision 2: an ambiguous email, where the right move is to flag it.
  6. Job aid: a three-step process to use before classifying any document.
Inside the activity

The review assignment

Fictional practice scenario

A company is investigating whether employees shared confidential Project Cedar pricing with an outside vendor during September 2026.

Review instructions

RMark Relevant if it discusses sharing Project Cedar pricing with an outside vendor during September 2026, even if it does not prove wrongdoing.
NMark Not relevant if it clearly concerns an unrelated topic.
FFlag for guidance if you cannot confidently apply these instructions.

Decision 1 · Review this email

From Morgan Lee, Account Manager

To Taylor Reed, Sales Operations

Date September 14, 2026

Subject Project Cedar pricing request

Taylor, our outside vendor, Northstar Supply, requested the confidential Project Cedar pricing spreadsheet. Before I send anything, can you confirm whether we have approval to share it? Morgan

✓RelevantIt discusses sharing Cedar pricing with a vendor in September. Asking permission doesn't make it irrelevant.

Decision 2 · What if the details are unclear?

Date September 21, 2026

Subject Follow-up

"Did you send those numbers to our contact? Please confirm before tomorrow's meeting."

No earlier messages or attachments. No project, no definition of "numbers," no named contact.

✓Flag for guidanceNot enough context to call it relevant or unrelated, so don't assume.

Job aid · Before you classify a document

  1. Check the instructions. Identify the topic, people, and dates in scope.
  2. Read for context. A matching keyword alone doesn't establish relevance, and a relevant document doesn't necessarily prove wrongdoing.
  3. Make a supported decision. Choose Relevant or Not relevant when the context supports it. Flag for guidance when you can't confidently apply the instructions.
Key design decisions

Why it's built this way

Five minutes, one skill

WhyNew reviewers are often onboarded quickly and under deadline. One short lesson that practices one core judgment fits into a ramp-up plan and can be repeated or spaced, rather than competing with a long platform course.

The first email targets a misconception

WhyThe email asks for permission and nothing has been sent, so it feels "innocent." A learner who equates relevance with wrongdoing marks it Not relevant. The feedback names that mistake directly: relevance and proof of wrongdoing are different judgments.

"Flag for guidance" is a correct answer, not a cop-out

WhyReliable review depends on knowing the limits of the instructions. Making escalation a graded, correct answer in Decision 2 teaches that asking is part of doing the job well, not a failure.

Content and context over keywords

WhyThe ambiguous email contains none of the scope terms but might still matter, and a keyword match alone wouldn't settle the first one either. Learners practice reading for meaning, which still matters when search or AI tools surface the documents.

Fictional, but realistic

WhyA made-up company, project, and people keep the practice free of real client data while preserving the details a reviewer actually weighs: sender, recipient, date, subject, and what's missing.

Measuring impact

How I would evaluate it in a real rollout

Learning

Decision accuracy

Results on each decision, especially how many learners mark the first email Not relevant, which shows how common the misconception is.

Behavior

Healthy flag rate

On a new reviewer's first assignment, track flags for guidance: too few suggests guessing, too many suggests the instructions or training need work.

Results

QC overturns

Compare the rate at which quality-control reviewers overturn new reviewers' relevance calls, before and after the lesson.
